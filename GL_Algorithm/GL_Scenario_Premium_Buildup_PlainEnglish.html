How one policy's premium is built

A shop with a liquor licence: premises and products cover, liquor liability, medical payments at $10,000, terrorism accepted. Written 17 September 2026; re-checked on 2 October 2026 by pricing this exact shop in the working rating engine (commit 630ba2f), which corrected four points below. Figures are for an Oklahoma antique shop with $1 million of sales under the rulebook that takes effect on 1 April 2027. The detailed version is GL_Scenario_Premium_Buildup.md.

You would expect six charges on this policy. There are four, and one of the things you expected to be a charge turns out not to be one.

Medical payments is not a charge

Every General Liability policy already includes $5,000 of medical payments cover. Asking for $10,000 does not add a line to the bill — it makes the premises and operations rate slightly more expensive — here $827 instead of $824. Nothing else on the policy changes. (Under today's rules it is a small separate charge folded into the same premium.)

So if someone asks "what did the medical payments cost", there is no number to point at. It is baked into another charge.

Liquor needs its own policy section

Corrected. ISO's rules switch each coverage on by the policy's line of business, and one section of a policy has one. Liquor liability cannot sit beside premises and products cover on the same section — added there, it prices at nothing. It is written as a second section of the same submission, and the two sections' totals are added together.

The coverages are priced the same way

Premises and operations, products, and liquor liability are each priced alike: start from ISO's figure for that trade, apply the insurer's own multiplier, adjust for the limits bought and the size and history of the business, then multiply by the exposure — the sales, payroll or whatever that trade is measured on. Here: premises $827, products $21.

Terrorism is a slice of the other three

The terrorism charge is not priced from scratch. It is a percentage of the premium already worked out for the other coverages — 0.4% here, so $3 on premises and $1 on products (the $1 is the minimum a real exposure can cost). Because the medical payments upgrade raises the premises charge, it can raise the terrorism charge on top of it.

Corrected: turning terrorism down does not always make the charge zero. If the policy carries an endorsement that caps or excludes some terrorism losses, ISO still prices a (smaller) terrorism charge.

It also means terrorism has to be calculated last. Nothing else on the policy depends on what comes before it in this way.

Adding it up

The charges are added together for the classification, then for the location, then for the policy section: $827 + $21 + $3 + $1 = $852, plus the liquor section.

One rule decides whether a coverage joins in: it must have produced a premium, and that premium must not be zero. A coverage priced at nothing and a coverage never bought look identical in the total — which is why, if there is real exposure but the maths comes to zero, the rules force the charge to $1 rather than leave it at nought.

The minimum premium depends on the date

Corrected. In the April 2027 rulebook ISO applies no minimum premium to these coverages, and the place on the policy that used to hold a top-up is switched off — anything put there is erased. A minimum has to be applied after the engine has priced the policy; if nobody does it, the policy simply prices below its own minimum and nothing objects.

Under the rulebook in force today it is the other way round: ISO compares the premium with the minimum itself and adds the shortfall. The same shop priced today comes to $939, not $852 — mostly because today's claims figure for the trade is higher.
