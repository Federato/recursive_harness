How one policy's premium is built

A shop with a liquor licence: premises and products cover, liquor liability, medical payments at $10,000, terrorism accepted. Written 17 September 2026. The detailed version is GL_Scenario_Premium_Buildup.md.

You would expect six charges on this policy. There are four, and one of the things you expected to be a charge turns out not to be one.

Medical payments is not a charge

Every General Liability policy already includes $5,000 of medical payments cover. Asking for $10,000 does not add a line to the bill — it makes the premises and operations rate slightly more expensive, by about seven tenths of one percent. Nothing else on the policy changes.

So if someone asks "what did the medical payments cost", there is no number to point at. It is baked into another charge.

Three coverages are priced the same way

Premises and operations, products, and liquor liability are each priced identically: take the claims cost ISO publishes for that trade, apply the insurer's own multiplier, adjust for the limits bought and the size and history of the business, then multiply by the exposure — the sales, payroll or whatever that trade is measured on.

Three separate charges, three times the same arithmetic.

Terrorism is a slice of the other three

The terrorism charge is not priced from scratch. It is a percentage of the premium already worked out for the other coverages. That has a quiet consequence: because the medical payments upgrade raised the premises charge, it also raised the terrorism charge on top of it. The $10,000 election costs a little more than it first appears.

It also means terrorism has to be calculated last. Nothing else on the policy depends on what comes before it in this way.

Adding it up

The four charges are added together for the classification, then for the location, then for the policy. That total is the premium.

One rule decides whether a coverage joins in: it must have produced a premium, and that premium must not be zero. A coverage priced at nothing and a coverage never bought look identical in the total — which is why, if there is real exposure but the maths comes to zero, the rules force the charge to $1 rather than leave it at nought.

The minimum premium is only half there

Every policy has minimum premiums, and ISO publishes them. It also provides a place on the policy to put the shortfall if the premium comes in under the minimum, and it will add that shortfall to the total.

What it does not do is the comparison. Nothing checks the premium against the minimum, works out the difference, or fills that box in. Somebody else has to — and if nobody does, the policy simply prices below its own minimum and nothing objects.
