How the two main General Liability premiums are built

Premises/Operations and Products/Completed Operations, in plain English. Written 17 September 2026. The detailed version is GL_PremOps_Prods_Premium_Trace.md.

These two coverages are the core of a General Liability policy. In ISO's rules they are built by the same program twice, with different field names swapped in. Whatever is true of one is true of the other.

Four steps from a filed rate to a dollar amount

It starts with ISO's loss cost — the raw cost of claims for that class of business. The carrier's own multiplier is applied to turn it into a base rate. That is the only place the carrier's pricing enters.

The base rate is then adjusted for everything specific to the account: the limits bought, the size of the risk, the package discount, the experience modification, the expense modification. That produces the final rate.

The final rate is multiplied by the exposure — the payroll, sales, floor area or whatever that class is rated on — and rounded to whole dollars. That is the premium.

A second number that is not money

Alongside the premium, each coverage also works out a basic limits premium. It uses the same base rate and exposure, but deliberately leaves out the limits charge and the account's modifications.

This second number travels all the way up to policy level and is stored there. It is never added to what the customer pays. It exists for statistical and regulatory reporting — it answers "what would this have cost at standard limits", which insurers must report.

This is worth knowing because anyone reading the data will find two premium figures per coverage and has to know which one is the bill.

How the premium reaches the account total

The premium is added up three times: first with everything else on that classification — terrorism, liquor, pollution, cyber and a dozen more — then with the other classifications at that location, then with every other location and policy-level charge. That final figure is the account premium.

One rule governs whether a coverage joins in at all: it must be marked as having produced a premium, and that premium must not be zero. A coverage priced at zero and a coverage that was never bought both add nothing, and look identical in the total.

Two things that surprise people

A tiny risk still costs a dollar. If the arithmetic produces zero but there is real exposure, the rules force the premium to $1. Zero is reserved for "not covered".

Minimum premiums are looked up but never applied. Both coverages find their minimum premium and then do nothing with it. If a minimum is meant to be enforced on these two coverages, it is not in ISO's filed rules — somebody else has to do it.
