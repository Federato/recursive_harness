Recursive Harness · GL Rating Engine

How the Rating Engine Was Built

From a shelf of ISO manuals to an engine checked against ISO's own rating service. October 21, 2026.

1Gathered ISO's rulebooks

We downloaded 975 ISO Commercial Lines manual PDFs for General Liability. 503 are rules manuals (how to rate) and 472 are loss cost manuals (the rates themselves). They cover 51 jurisdictions from 2020 to 2027.

We read them first and wrote a plain-English build plan. Reading them showed the national manual can't produce a price by itself: the real numbers sit in each state's pages, and rule numbers get reused between editions.

2Added ISO's electronic content (ERC)

Next came ISO's machine-readable rating files, the ERC packages, starting with 570 of them. We built spreadsheets linking these files to the manuals: which edition replaces which, and which loss costs feed which ERC files.

ERC became the engine's source of truth, and the PDFs became the check against it.

What's in an ERC package: each one is a ZIP for one state and edition. It holds the rules (step-by-step premium calculations), rate tables (loss costs, factors, limits), the layout of a submission, lists of allowed values, form and ratebook listings, and metadata. Alaska's October 2026 GL package has 41 rules and 82 rate tables.

3Built the Excel raters

Alongside the engine, we built two spreadsheet raters from the same ERC content:

  • General Liability 1.03: Premises/Operations and Products/Completed Ops, with Med Pay, Product Withdrawal, OCP, TRIA, deductibles and Additional Insureds. 52 jurisdictions.
  • CFRater 1.01.08 (Commercial Property): Building, Contents, Business Income and Special Class, the cause-of-loss forms, coinsurance and deductibles. 46 states plus Puerto Rico.

The harness reads the Property rater directly. It turns a risk entered in the spreadsheet into an ISO request, then prices it three ways. Excel, ISO and the engine agreed on 42 of 42.

4Built the engine in six stages

  1. Measured exactly how ISO's files are meant to be read.
  2. Built an interpreter that reads ISO's formulas.
  3. Built the kernel, the core that turns them into a premium.
  4. Used ISO's own field layout instead of designing our own.
  5. Built a spreadsheet of allowed values for each field (GL-Submission-Fields.xlsx).
  6. Built the screen where you enter a risk and see the premium.

All six were done in two days. Before any live connection, 49 of 49 usable sample answers from ISO's files matched to the penny.

5Connected to ISO's rating service (RaaS) Aug 13

We copied the connection approach from an earlier project and wrote a small client. It logs in with a client ID and secret, sends the risk to ISO, and gets ISO's premium back. The login details come from environment variables, not the code.

On the first run, ISO answered for 50 jurisdictions, and all 50 matched us on the premium and every line. ISO also confirmed it used the same edition we did. Puerto Rico didn't answer because it isn't on the subscription.

6Used ISO as the referee

After go-live, every engine change was checked against ISO's answer before and after, usually with 2 to 10 calls. Varying the business (size, limits, class, experience and schedule rating) turned up real defects, and each got a tracking number and a fix.

ISO's answers also showed we were missing 16 newer state filings, which we then added. Calls are capped at 250 a day and logged, and ISO's answers are stored and reused.

7How Claude learned from ISO and began correcting itself

Every disagreement with ISO became a lesson, not just a fix. Claude works in a simple loop:

  1. Ask: send the same risk to ISO and to our engine.
  2. Compare: every line, not just the total.
  3. Trace: find the exact rule, table or step that differs.
  4. Fix and re-check: propose the fix, get a go, then confirm it with ISO.
  5. Generalize: write a rule that prevents the whole kind of mistake.

For example, ISO showed it rounds halves up (4 of 4). And a New York terrorism test that "passed" turned out to be testing nothing: the harness caught its own mistake. Two specialist reviewers check every finding, because not every difference is our error.

8Expanded the scope

  • Commercial Property: built in 28 numbered pieces, each with its own pass/fail check.
  • Carrier deviations: a carrier's own filed changes layered on top of ISO's rates, starting with UFGAL. They're priced by ISO too, and 3 of 3 matched.
  • Filings intake: carrier filings are stored by their filing number (SERFF) and logged.

9Made it usable and explainable

  • A test console that runs planned batches of ISO calls, plus an Upload & Compare page.
  • Runnable notebooks that explain the engine file by file, and visual maps of how each rating is calculated.
  • Specialist helpers that answer questions from the code or the manuals.
  • Plain-language guides, a running build log, a backlog grouped into work batches, and mockups for the next redesign.

10Where it is now In progress

We're mapping the full chain of ISO's calculations as a "compute graph" in a separate test project, ISOComputeGraph, and simplifying the main app. Commercial Property chunk 28 and the forms backlog are still open.
